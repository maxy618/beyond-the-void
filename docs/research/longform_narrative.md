# Long-form sci-fi exploration narrative — knowledge gates, companions, endings

## Question
How do acclaimed long-form exploration narratives (Outer Wilds, Subnautica, Return of the
Obra Dinn, Heaven's Vault, Citizen Sleeper, Mass Effect 2, Sunless Sea, Disco Elysium)
structure knowledge-gated mysteries without cutscene dumps, pace companion arcs, pay off
endings against accumulated evidence/choice, and use procedural or semi-authored spaces for
authored beats? What failure modes (mystery fatigue, lore dumps, false choices, unearned
twists) are documented? What can a small 2D team actually build?

## Bottom line
- **The canonical structure is a "web of curiosities", not a quest list.** Outer Wilds'
  designer wrote it down in his MFA thesis: the world presents POIs that communicate
  *affordances* ("one tornado of many lifts objects; one pushes down"), and it is "left up to
  the player" to chain them into deduction; curiosity is formally defined as exploration
  that "starts with a question" whose answer is the reward.
- **Knowledge is the only durable currency.** Outer Wilds: consciousness is what survives the
  loop, so "memories and knowledge are the only things that could possibly persist" — the
  whole progression economy. The climax was deliberately redesigned from "stop the ending" to
  "understand why it ends".
- **Evidence-completion endings exist and are shippable at tiny scale:** Obra Dinn = 60
  fates (name + cause of death or present location) through a logbook and one pocket watch;
  Sunless Sea = choose an Ambition at game start, retire when its requirements are met
  (three visible ambitions + three hidden ones, each its own ending).
- **Companion pacing = investment unlocked at a mid-game trigger** (Mass Effect 2: after
  Horizon, each squadmate gets one personal-loyalty mission unlocking a power/outfit and
  changing the finale) or **nightly-cycle social systems** (Citizen Sleeper: built as a
  "minimum viable version" toolset able to ship episodes "reasonably fast" — a month each).
- **Language as puzzle, world as the exam:** Heaven's Vault makes 1000+ glyphs of a fictional
  language learnable in-game, with correctable guesses so "every player's understanding of
  the language will develop differently" (developer quote via hands-on coverage).
- **Failure as text** is a shippable, cheap device: Disco Elysium's first night's dream
  dialogue with your own dead body — "everything is hopeless and he will inevitably fail" —
  keeps playing; the design interview documents systems (24 skills, ~1M words, Thought
  Cabinet iterated 12 times) designed to make failure discussable rather than terminal.

## Analysis
**One shape keeps recurring: a question-led object web.** Outer Wilds (thesis) and Obra Dinn
(logbook) both begin from *player-held questions*, and both resolve through a fixed in-world
book/board the player maintains. The core insight for a 2D game: the *evidence board is the
save file of understanding* — it is cheap UI and 100% authored payload.

**Pacing knowledge, not missions.** The writing design documented for Outer Wilds (many
small mysteries rewarded more frequently) is the direct counter to mystery fatigue; the
"No pure lore" rule keeps every line load-bearing. Heaven's Vault pushes the same idea into
a skill (language) rather than geography.

**Fail states as content.** Disco Elysium and Subnautica both thread failure into texture:
DE makes despair itself a findable object; Subnautica's corrupted coordinates are an authored
emotional beat encoded as a system message. Neither requires cutscenes.

**Endings that read as fair:** sun-set retirement in Sunless Sea and the 60-fates form in
Obra Dinn make the ending an *inspection of what you did*, mirroring Kelsey Beachum's
"understanding, not stopping" ending. The ME2 loyalty model shows how arcs can be paced by a
mid-game investment trigger with mechanical consequences at the finale.

**Failure modes visible in the sources:** mystery fatigue arises when rewards are sparse (in
Outer Wilds' own countermeasure quote); lore dumps are what OW's "no pure lore" rule exists
to prevent: every text must be "valuable, important content that should not be skipped";
false choices are visible in systems where the mechanical "choice" has no consequence — for
this pass, that claim is carried by our NMS file (Atlas ending "do nothing or create a star",
both results identical except one map feature) — cross-referenced, not new evidence here.

## Implications for Beyond the Void
*Evidence says:*
1. **Ship a playable evidence board** (like Obra Dinn's logbook / OW's ship computer): for a
   portrait screen it can be a single scrollable card list where each planet visit adds one
   entry; the finale reads the board.
2. **Questions, not quest markers, trigger exploration** on the first planet: one "object
   affordance" (e.g., one cave draws air; one ruin hums back) teaches the verb set without
   saying "go here".
3. **Small mysteries frequently rewarded** — budget ~1 new text/knowledge beat per 5–10
   minutes; this is Outer Wilds' own stated countermeasure for mystery fatigue.
4. **No pure-lore text:** every dialogue line either teaches a mechanic, hints a location, or
   carries consequence. Enforce as content rule for localization files.
5. **Ending as inspection:** define an explicit countable ambition early (Sunless Sea) whose
   completion state is the finale; for a procedural first planet, the ambition can be "bring
   the fragment home" with a counted checklist.
6. **A tiny toolset that ships content in a month** (Citizen Sleeper) is a tested
   ceiling for our scope: one author-tool for typed beats (dialogue objects bound to POI
   types) rather than bespoke scripting.
7. **Companion/gift arcs** can borrow the ME2 trigger model: one investment beat per
   companion, timed at mid-story, changing a finale count.

*My judgement (not sourced):* for our narrative, the sustainable minimum is **one authored
mystery chain (5–7 beats) + one persistent evidence board + one ambition checklist**; three
parallels is a live-service scope we cannot man.
